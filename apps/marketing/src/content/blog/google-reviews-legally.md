Getting more Google reviews means asking happy customers at the right moment, through a channel they'll actually answer, without crossing the one line Google enforces hardest: paying for reviews or trading a discount for one. Most advice stops at "just ask more often" and skips that rule entirely, along with the newer trust numbers that decide whether the reviews you already have are even doing their job. Here's what Google's own policy actually says, what the 2026 data shows about how many reviews and how recent they need to be, and how to automate the ask without a human forgetting to send it.

## What Counts as Actually Asking the Right Way?

Asking the right way means requesting a review through a direct Google link or QR code, at a moment when the customer just had a good experience, with nothing offered in return. Google's own review policy spells this out plainly: businesses can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code to leave a review, full stop. What it explicitly bans is the next step most owners reach for instinctively, offering a discount, a gift or any other incentive in exchange for a review, changing a review or removing a negative one. Google calls that "fake and misleading content" and treats it as a policy violation, not a gray area.

That single rule quietly disqualifies a huge share of the "review hacks" floating around small business forums: the sign by the register offering 10% off for a five-star review, the raffle entry for anyone who posts one, the gift card tucked into a thank-you email tied to leaving feedback. Every one of those ties a reward to the act of reviewing, and every one of those is exactly what the policy forbids. The businesses that get flagged or have reviews stripped almost never think they're cheating, they think they're being generous, and that's exactly why the rule catches so many well-meaning owners instead of only the obvious scammers.

The safer version of "being generous" is separating the two asks completely. Thank a customer for the job and, separately, maybe send them a loyalty discount for their next visit with no mention of reviews anywhere in that message. Then, in a different message sent at a different time, with no reward attached and no reference to the discount, ask if they'd be willing to leave a review. Keeping those two threads from ever touching is what keeps the goodwill intact without tripping the rule that gets reviews deleted months later once Google's systems catch the pattern.

## When Should You Actually Ask for a Review?

The best moment to ask is right after the customer has had the clear, positive outcome they came for, while it's still fresh enough to write about in two sentences. For a service business, that's typically within a few hours of the job finishing, not days later once the memory of exactly what went well has faded into a vague "yeah it was fine." For a dental practice or clinic, it's right after a good visit, not buried in a monthly newsletter the patient skims past.

Timing matters because the review itself gets more specific and more useful the sooner it's written. A review sent two hours after a plumber fixes a leak mentions the plumber by name and the exact problem solved. The same customer asked three weeks later writes "good service, would recommend", which is a fine review but a far less persuasive one for the next person reading it. Specificity is what makes a review actually move someone toward booking, and specificity decays fast.

This is also where most small teams lose the thread, not because they don't know the rule but because remembering to send the ask, for every job, every day, competes with everything else on their plate. A technician who just finished a water heater install is thinking about the next job, not opening an app to fire off a review request. That gap between "we know we should ask" and "someone actually sent it" is where most of the missed reviews live, and it's a scheduling problem as much as a willingness problem.

## How Many Reviews Do You Actually Need to Matter?

You need more than most business owners assume, and the exact numbers come from BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey rather than guesswork. Across that survey, 97% of consumers read reviews before deciding whether to use a local business, which makes reviews closer to a requirement than a nice-to-have for anyone competing on a search results page or a map listing.

The volume threshold is steeper than it used to be. 47% of consumers won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews at all, and only 9% are willing to use one with five or fewer. A business sitting at eight or nine reviews isn't just behind, it's invisible to nearly half the people who'd otherwise consider it. Star rating adds another filter on top: 68% of consumers will only use a business with four or more stars, up from 55% just the year before, which means the bar for "good enough to click" keeps rising even as review volume across the internet keeps growing.

Freshness is the part almost nobody tracks. 74% of consumers specifically seek out reviews written in the last three months, treating anything older as a weaker signal of what working with you is like right now. A business that collected forty great reviews two years ago and stopped is, in the eyes of most searchers, running on a reputation that's already gone stale. The practical takeaway isn't "get reviews once". It's building a steady drip, every week, every month, so the newest review is never more than a few weeks old.

## Should You Ask by Text, Email, or Both?

Text generally gets answered faster, but email still has a place when the ask includes more context than fits in a text message. A review request text gets opened within minutes in most cases, the same reason a missed call text back works so well for capturing a lead, because people check texts almost immediately and emails whenever they get around to it, which for a promotional-looking subject line can be never.

The practical answer most businesses land on is channel-matching rather than picking one forever. If the customer gave you a phone number during the job, like a plumber or an electrician would, text wins on speed and open rate. If the relationship runs mostly through email already, like a law firm or an accounting practice corresponding over a case or a filing, an email that reads like a real note from the person who helped them tends to outperform a text that feels oddly informal for that relationship. The common mistake is treating the channel choice as a brand decision instead of matching it to wherever that specific customer already expects to hear from you.

## How Do You Know the Review Requests Are Actually Working?

You know by tracking what share of the requests you send turn into an actual review, broken out by channel, rather than just counting total reviews and assuming the system behind them is healthy. A business that sends eighty review requests a month and gets six reviews back has a conversion problem worth fixing, even if six new reviews a month feels like progress compared with none.

Splitting that number by channel usually turns up the real story. A text-based request might convert at close to a third of sends, because the customer sees it within minutes and the link is one tap away, while an email version of the same ask sitting unopened in a promotions folder converts in the low single digits. Averaging those together into one blended "review request conversion rate" hides which channel is actually doing the work and which one is quietly wasting every send.

The fix mirrors the channel-matching idea from earlier: once you know texts convert noticeably better for a given customer base, shift more of the volume there instead of treating email as the default just because it's easier to template. Revisit the number every few months rather than setting it once, since a wording change, a new technician's personal touch on the message, or even the season can move the conversion rate enough to be worth noticing.

## Should You Automate the Ask, or Send It Yourself Every Time?

Automating the ask solves the real problem, which is that remembering to send a well-timed request for every single customer isn't something a busy team reliably does by hand, week after week. The fix isn't a generic monthly blast to everyone on a customer list. It's a trigger tied to the actual moment that matters: a job marked complete, an appointment closed out, an invoice paid, firing off the review request within that same window while the experience is still fresh enough to write about specifically.

[Home service businesses](/industries/home-services) are a clean example of why this matters more than it sounds like it should. A three-person crew finishing six jobs a day has no spare minute to log into a review tool after every stop, so the honest choice is between an automated send and no consistent send at all. The same gap shows up for [dental practices](/industries/dental), where a packed schedule means nobody at the front desk is pausing between patients to manually trigger a review request, even though right after a good visit is exactly when that patient would say yes.

## What Do You Do When a Bad Review Shows Up Anyway?

You respond to it, specifically and without getting defensive, because the response matters as much to future readers as the review itself. A calm, specific reply, naming what happened and what you did or will do about it, shows the next person reading that review that a real human is actually paying attention to feedback rather than ignoring it. Responding within a day or two is enough; there's no evidence waiting a week quietly makes things worse beyond the obvious frustration of leaving someone unanswered.

Don't argue the facts in public and don't offer anything that reads like a bribe to change or delete the review, since that circles right back to the incentive rule covered earlier. If the review violates an actual Google policy, is fake, is from someone who was never a customer, or contains content that shouldn't be there, you can flag it for removal through Google's own reporting process. What you can't do is pay or barter your way out of a legitimate bad review.

The better move is usually fixing whatever the review describes and letting that fix speak through your next batch of fresher, better reviews instead of chasing the one bad one. A single two-star review sitting next to thirty recent four- and five-star reviews reads as one unhappy customer in an otherwise strong track record. The same two-star review sitting almost alone, next to three reviews from a year ago, reads as a real warning sign, which is one more reason the steady weekly drip from earlier in this piece matters more than any individual response to a single complaint.

## Google Reviews: The Questions People Actually Ask

**Is it against Google's policy to offer a discount for a review?**
Yes. Google's review policy explicitly prohibits offering incentives, including discounts or gifts, in exchange for posting, changing or removing a review. It calls that fake and misleading content, and it's one of the more actively enforced rules in the policy.

**How many Google reviews does a small business actually need?**
Enough to clear the thresholds that filter out the rest. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 47% of consumers won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, so twenty is a realistic first target rather than an arbitrary round number.

**Do older reviews still count, or do they lose value over time?**
They lose weight with a lot of searchers. 74% of consumers specifically look for reviews written in the last three months, which means a strong set of reviews from two years ago reads as outdated to most people checking right now.

**Is it better to ask for a review by text or by email?**
Text usually gets opened and answered faster, especially right after a service visit. Email still works well when the relationship already runs through email, like with a law firm or an accounting client.

**Can an automated system send review requests without a business owner's input?**
It can send the routine ask automatically once it's set up, triggered by something real like a completed job, but a well-built setup still keeps a human approving anything beyond that default message before it goes out under the business's name.

**What should you do if a negative review is fake or from a non-customer?**
Report it through Google's own review-flagging process rather than trying to argue with it publicly or offer something to get it removed, which risks violating the same incentive policy that governs positive reviews.
