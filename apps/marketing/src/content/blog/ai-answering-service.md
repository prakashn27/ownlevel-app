An AI answering service is software that picks up a business's incoming calls, understands what the caller wants in normal spoken language, and then answers a question, books an appointment, or passes a message along, all without a person on the other end. It runs on $0.30 to $0.50 per call on average, against $6 to $7.68 for a human agent handling the same call, which is why so many small businesses are looking at it right now. The number that gets buried in most vendor pitches is what happens the one time in twenty it mishears a name or books the wrong slot, and whether anyone catches that before the customer does.

## What Does an AI Answering Service Actually Do?

At the core, it answers the phone so a missed call stops meaning a missed customer. The software picks up on the first or second ring, greets the caller, and figures out why they called: a question about hours or pricing, a request to book or reschedule, a complaint, or something it genuinely can't handle. For the questions it already knows, it answers directly from whatever script or knowledge base the business set up. For a booking request, it checks the real calendar and offers open slots instead of promising a time that's already taken.

The harder calls get routed, not guessed at. A caller asking about a billing dispute, a medical concern, or anything emotionally loaded gets passed to a person, either immediately or through a callback request, because that's exactly the kind of call where a wrong guess costs more than it saves. A well-built answering service treats "I don't know, let me get you a person" as a correct answer, not a failure. The ones that pretend to handle everything are the ones that end up overpromising to a caller who then shows up expecting something the business never agreed to.

## How Does an AI Answering Service Work, Technically?

The plumbing behind it is simpler than it sounds. A business forwards its existing phone number to the AI service, the same way call forwarding has worked for decades, so nothing changes from the caller's side and the business keeps its own number. When a call lands, speech recognition turns the audio into text in real time, and a language model reads that text to figure out intent, not just matching keywords but actually following what the caller is asking for even if they phrase it in an unusual way.

Once it understands the request, it checks whatever system it needs: the calendar for a booking, a knowledge base for a question, a CRM record for an existing customer's history. Then it responds out loud, in a voice built to sound close to natural rather than robotic, and takes the action the caller asked for if it's confident enough to do so. The last step is the one that matters for the business owner: a text or email summary lands in their inbox right after the call, showing what was said and what the system did about it, so nobody's flying blind about what just happened on their phone line while they were busy with something else.

## What Does an AI Answering Service Actually Cost?

Real numbers vary more by vendor than most comparisons admit, so it's worth looking at a few side by side instead of trusting one company's pricing page. Trillet prices its entry AI receptionist plan at $49 a month with 150 voice minutes included, then $0.20 for each additional minute, a structure built around actual call volume rather than a flat seat price. Nextiva's industry comparison puts typical AI call handling at $0.30 to $0.50 per call, against $6 to $7.68 per call for a human agent, and notes that a full-time receptionist carries a median salary of $38,010 a year before benefits and taxes, with AI answering subscriptions across the market generally landing between $30 and $300 a month depending on call volume and features.

| Option | Typical cost | What you're paying for |
|---|---|---|
| AI answering service | $0.30 to $0.50 per call, or $30 to $300/month subscription | Software, no breaks, no sick days |
| Human answering service | $6 to $7.68 per call | A live person, judgment on hard calls |
| In-house receptionist | $38,010/year median salary, before benefits | Full-time presence, institutional knowledge |

The cheapest number on the page is never the full story. A subscription with a low minute cap gets expensive fast once call volume climbs past what the plan assumes, and a business that pays per call needs to actually track how many calls it's fielding before picking a plan, not guess from memory. The honest comparison isn't AI versus a receptionist's full salary, since most small businesses were never going to hire a dedicated receptionist anyway; it's AI versus a voicemail that nobody calls back, which is the real alternative for a huge share of missed calls today.

## When Does It Actually Make Sense, and When Should You Skip It?

It earns its cost fastest for businesses with real call volume outside their open hours: a home services company fielding emergency calls at 9pm, a dental office getting voicemails over the weekend, a salon that loses bookings every time the front desk is mid-haircut and can't pick up. In those cases the alternative isn't a human picking up instead, it's nobody picking up at all, so the AI is competing against silence, not against a person.

It makes far less sense for a business with low call volume to begin with, since a $49 to $300 monthly fee for a trickle of calls a human could handle in a few minutes a day is paying for capacity nobody needs yet. It's also the wrong tool for anything emotionally loaded as the front door of the business: a funeral home's intake line, a crisis line, or any situation where the caller needs to feel heard by an actual person before anything else happens. And it isn't built for emergencies. Trillet is explicit that its service "is not an emergency-response service," which is true industry-wide, not a quirk of one vendor; a caller with a real emergency needs 911 or a direct line to a person, not a system that might still be figuring out intent while the clock runs.

The clearest tell is whether the business already loses real revenue to calls nobody answers. A landscaping company that only takes ten calls a week doesn't have a missed-call problem worth solving with software. A plumbing company fielding forty after-hours calls a month, half of which currently go to voicemail and never get a callback, has a problem that's actively costing it booked jobs, and that's the gap an answering service is built to close. The same logic applies to a law firm's intake line or a salon that's short-staffed at the front desk during peak hours: the question isn't whether AI sounds impressive, it's whether a human picking up every one of those calls was ever realistic in the first place.

## What Happens When the AI Gets a Call Wrong?

Every vendor's marketing leads with the calls it handles well. The honest question is what happens on the call it doesn't, and most of the pages selling this software barely touch it. Trillet's own documentation is more candid than most: it cautions that "not every requested slot is successfully booked," which means a caller can walk away thinking they have an appointment that was never actually confirmed on the calendar. Speech recognition still misreads names, especially uncommon ones, and misunderstands requests phrased in a way the system didn't anticipate, the same failure mode any voice technology has struggled with since the first automated phone tree.

The businesses that avoid getting burned by this don't trust the system blind. They get a record of exactly what the AI heard and did on each call, checked soon after the call ends rather than days later, and they start with a narrow slice of call types instead of handing over the whole phone line on day one. [Speed to respond](/blog/speed-to-lead) matters, but speed without a way to catch the rare miss just means the mistake reaches the customer faster. That's the same reasoning behind [why an AI agent needs a human to approve its outbound actions](/blog/ai-agent-vs-chatbot) in any automated system, not just a phone line: the agent does the work, and a person still gets a quick look at what it's about to do before it reaches a real customer, so the one time in twenty it's wrong gets caught instead of shipped.

Most vendor pages treat this review step as an afterthought, something you set up once during onboarding and then forget about. In practice it works better as an ongoing habit: a quick daily or weekly glance at the call log, not a one-time setup checklist. A business that reviews its first two weeks of calls closely, then spot-checks after that once the system has proven itself on the calls it's actually getting, ends up with a much better sense of where its particular AI setup tends to struggle than one that never looks at the log at all. That pattern, trust built gradually through actual review rather than assumed on day one, is the difference between an answering service that quietly improves and one that quietly accumulates small mistakes nobody ever catches.

## Is a Text-Based Agent a Safer Place to Start?

Nearly every page written about this topic assumes the only option is a full voice AI answering the live call, and that framing skips the alternative that's actually simpler to trust: a system that notices the missed call and sends a text back immediately instead of trying to have the conversation out loud. [Missed call text back](/blog/missed-call-text-back) works on the same core idea, catch the contact before it goes cold, but it swaps a live phone conversation for a written one, and that swap matters more than it sounds like it should.

A text draft sits there, visible, before it goes out. There's no live caller waiting on hold while a person reads it, and no misheard word baked into an audio stream that already happened by the time anyone reviews it. A business owner or a staffer can glance at what the system is about to send, catch a wrong detail in two seconds, and either approve it or fix it, the exact kind of check that's much harder to run on a phone call that's already over by the time a transcript lands in an inbox. It also sidesteps the setup cost of rewiring a phone system: a text-back flow sits on top of the number you already have without touching how calls get answered at all, and the per-message cost runs well under what a voice-minute plan charges once a business is fielding any real volume.

None of this makes a text-based agent strictly better than a voice answering service for every business. A caller who wants to talk through a complicated booking still wants a voice on the other end, and some customers simply won't text back. [Qualifying who's calling matters as much as how fast you respond](/blog/ai-lead-qualification), whichever channel handles it. But for a business that's nervous about handing a live phone call to software it hasn't tested yet, starting with a text-based reply, reviewed before it goes out, is the lower-risk way to find out whether automated response actually works for its customers before committing to a full voice setup.

## Should Your Business Build or Buy This?

Nobody serious builds an AI answering service from scratch for one business. The speech recognition, the calendar integration, the escalation logic, all of it is a real engineering project that takes months and then needs ongoing maintenance as the underlying models and the business's own offerings change. Every option discussed here, Trillet, Nextiva's comparison set, and the text-based alternative, is a platform a business rents rather than builds, and that's the right call for anyone whose actual business is dental care, home repair, or legal work, not software engineering.

The harder decision is who you rent it from and how much control you keep over what it does on your behalf. A vendor that hands over a dashboard and a monthly invoice under its own brand is fine for a single business picking its own tools. An agency managing this for a roster of clients runs into a different problem: reselling someone else's branded tool makes the agency look like a middleman, which is the exact issue [white-label platforms exist to solve](/blog/white-label-saas-cost) by putting the agency's own name on the invoice and the login screen instead.

## What to Decide Before You Turn an AI Loose on Your Phone Line

The real decision isn't whether the technology works, since the call volume math already answers that for most businesses with real traffic outside business hours. It's how much you're willing to risk on the calls it gets wrong, and whether you've set up a way to catch those before a customer feels the effect. A business running low call volume, or one where a caller needs to feel heard by a real person immediately, should skip this entirely rather than paying for capacity it doesn't need. Everyone else should start narrow, keep a record of what the system actually did on each call, and decide only after watching real calls whether voice, text, or a mix of both fits how their customers actually reach out.

## AI Answering Services: The Questions People Actually Ask

**What is an AI answering service, in plain terms?**
It's software that answers a business's phone calls, understands what the caller wants using normal spoken language, and then answers questions, books appointments, or passes the call to a person, without anyone on staff picking up.

**How much does an AI answering service cost per month?**
Subscription plans across the market generally run $30 to $300 a month depending on call volume, and per-call pricing from vendors like Trillet starts around $49 a month for 150 minutes, then $0.20 for each extra minute. Per-call cost alone, without a subscription, tends to land between $0.30 and $0.50.

**Can an AI answering service actually book appointments?**
Yes, by checking a real calendar and offering open slots rather than a canned time, though it isn't flawless, since vendors themselves caution that not every requested slot gets successfully confirmed. A business should check its bookings rather than assume every call that mentions scheduling actually landed on the calendar.

**What happens if the AI misunderstands a caller?**
It can misread a name or misjudge what someone meant, the same failure any voice technology has always had. The businesses that handle this well review a record of what the AI heard and did soon after each call, instead of assuming every interaction went the way it was supposed to.

**Does an AI answering service handle sensitive information like health details safely?**
That depends entirely on the vendor's specific plan, not on AI answering services as a category. Some entry-level plans explicitly exclude protected health information unless a separate agreement is signed, so a business in a health-adjacent field needs to check that in writing before assuming any plan covers it.

**Should a small business start with voice AI or text-based automation?**
A business nervous about live calls going to software it hasn't tested is usually better off starting with a text-based reply to a missed call, since a written draft can be reviewed before it's sent in a way a finished phone call can't be. Voice makes more sense once a business has real call volume outside business hours and has already seen automated response work on the lower-risk channel first.
