Lead qualification questions are the handful of things you need to know about a new inquiry before you decide whether to chase it: what the problem actually is, who gets to say yes, how soon they need it solved and whether they can actually pay for it. Most lists of these questions assume a person is asking them one at a time on a call. The real question for a small business today is different: which of these can go out automatically by text the moment a lead shows up, and which ones genuinely need a human voice on the other end before you can trust the answer.

## What Are Lead Qualification Questions, and Why Do They Matter?

Lead qualification questions are the short set of things you ask a new inquiry to find out whether they're worth a rep's time, before anyone books a call or drives to an estimate. A homeowner who fills out a quote form at 10pm might be ready to sign this week, or might be three months out from even picking a contractor. Without asking, you can't tell the difference, and treating both leads the same wastes either your fastest follow-up or your rep's afternoon.

The questions matter because the cost of skipping them isn't zero, it's just invisible. A rep who calls every single lead with the same urgency ends up spending twenty minutes on someone who was never going to buy this quarter, while a genuinely ready buyer sits in the same queue getting the same generic callback. [Lead qualification](/blog/ai-lead-qualification) as a system is the scoring and routing logic built around these answers. The questions themselves are the actual script, the words a rep or an automated reply uses to get the information that scoring depends on.

Get the questions wrong and the scoring on top of them doesn't matter. A system that asks "are you interested in our services?" learns almost nothing, because nearly everyone says yes to a question phrased that openly. The questions that actually separate a real buyer from someone comparison shopping are more specific than that, and they hold up whether a human is asking them on the phone or a text message is asking them the moment the lead comes in.

## Which Framework Should You Use, BANT or Something Simpler?

BANT is the framework most people already know without naming it: budget, authority, need, timeline. IBM built it decades ago to help a huge sales force quickly tell a serious prospect from someone who'd never actually sign, and it spread from there because it still does that job for almost any business selling something with a real price tag.

The four categories hold up for a dental practice, a roofing company or an insurance agency just as well as they held up for enterprise software. Budget tells you if this person can actually afford what you're selling. Authority tells you if you're talking to the person who decides, or someone who has to go check with a spouse, a property manager or a business partner first. Need tells you whether the problem is real and specific, or just a vague "might be interested someday". Timeline tells you whether this is a this-week decision or a someday decision, which changes how hard you follow up and how fast.

You don't need a fancier framework than this for most small businesses, and reaching for something more elaborate usually just adds steps nobody follows under real call volume. The newer variations, swapping "authority" for "committee" or adding a fifth category for competition, solve problems that mostly show up in long enterprise sales cycles with multiple stakeholders. A homeowner deciding on a water heater or a patient booking a cleaning doesn't need five categories, just four asked well.

## What Are the Core Questions to Ask Before You Book Someone?

The core questions map directly onto those four categories, phrased the way an actual person talks rather than the way a sales training deck phrases them. A script built around these five questions covers budget, authority, need and timeline without sounding like an interrogation:

1. What's going on, specifically, that's making you look into this now?
2. Have you dealt with this before, or tried to fix it yourself first?
3. Do you have a rough budget in mind, or a sense of what similar work has cost before?
4. Is this your call to make, or does someone else need to weigh in first?
5. Is this something you want handled this week, or are you still just exploring options?

Each one does a specific job. The first question surfaces the real need instead of a vague interest, and the answer to it usually tells you more than anything else on the list, because a specific, urgent problem answers itself differently than "just curious what you guys offer". The second catches whether this is a first-time inquiry or someone who's already tried and failed, which changes how much convincing they actually need. The third gets at budget without the awkwardness of asking for a number outright, since most people will tell you what similar work has cost even when they won't commit to a figure for yours. The fourth surfaces authority before you waste a presentation on someone who has to run it by a partner anyway. The fifth gets timeline without the push of "when do you want to buy", which tends to make people defensive.

None of this needs to feel like a checklist to the person answering it. The best version of this script reads like a text from someone who's actually curious what's going on, not a form with blanks to fill in. That distinction is also exactly where it starts to matter whether a human or an automated message is the one asking.

## Which of These Questions Can an AI Agent Ask by Text, and Which Need a Human?

The need and timeline questions are the safest ones to automate by text, because the answers are mostly factual and low-stakes to get slightly wrong. An AI agent texting back the moment a lead comes in, "thanks for reaching out, what's going on with the water heater, is it leaking or just not heating?" gets a real answer most people are happy to type out, and a wrong guess about timeline or symptom just means one more clarifying text, not a damaged relationship.

Budget and authority sit in murkier territory. Asking about budget by text works fine when it's phrased softly, something like "do you have a rough range in mind, just so we quote you something realistic?" People answer that one honestly over text about as often as they would out loud. Authority is where it gets harder to automate well, because the honest answer sometimes comes with context a script can't read, a hesitation, a "well, technically my wife handles this", a tone that signals the real decision-maker isn't the one typing. A human on a call catches that in a way a text thread usually can't.

This is also where the human-approval piece matters more than most explanations of AI-driven qualification let on. An agent reading a lead's answers and drafting the next question, or drafting the handoff to a rep, is doing real work fast. What it shouldn't do unsupervised is decide on its own that a borderline answer means "not worth pursuing" and let that lead quietly drop, because a model misreading a terse answer as disinterest costs you a sale with no one ever noticing it happened. The setups that hold up treat the agent's read of an answer as a draft a person can override, not a verdict that ends the conversation. The agent asks the questions and drafts the next step. A human still signs off before a lead gets dropped or before a reply promising a price or a timeline actually goes out under the business's name.

## What Do These Questions Look Like Across Different Businesses?

[Home service businesses](/industries/home-services) get the most value from automating the need and timeline questions by text, because a tech on a roof can't stop to ask them and a same-day leak doesn't wait for someone to be free. "Is this an emergency or can it wait a day or two?" sent the second an inquiry comes in sorts a flooding basement from a routine gutter cleaning before a human ever looks at the message, and that sort alone is most of the value.

[Dental practices](/industries/dental) lean harder on the authority and need questions, since a lot of inquiries come from a parent asking on behalf of a kid, or a patient unsure whether their insurance actually covers what they're asking about. A front desk that's buried in a waiting room benefits from a text that's already asked "is this for you or for someone else, and do you know if you're in-network with us" before a human has to pick up the thread.

[Real estate agents](/industries/real-estate) get the most mileage out of timeline and budget, since the difference between someone touring next weekend and someone browsing listings six months out changes everything about how hard an agent should push and how fast. [Insurance agencies](/industries/insurance-agencies) rely on need and a fit check almost more than budget, since a quote request from outside a coverage area needs to get screened out immediately rather than worked by an agent who finds out forty minutes into a call that the deal was never possible in the first place.

What's consistent across all four is that the same five questions apply, just with different weight on which one matters most for that business. Nobody needs a custom framework per industry. They need the same core script, tuned for which answer actually predicts whether that specific business can help.

## What Happens After a Lead Answers These Questions?

A good set of answers should route the lead somewhere specific, not just sit in a spreadsheet as five new data points. A lead whose answers check every box, clear need, real authority, workable budget, this-week timeline, should go straight to a booking link or a same-day callback task, because making that lead wait for a human to review five answers that already say "ready" just reintroduces the delay you automated the questions to avoid.

A lead with a mixed set of answers, real need but no clear authority yet, or a tight timeline but a vague budget, is where a human actually earns their time. That's the lead worth a rep's attention precisely because the automated questions surfaced something worth digging into, not because the system failed to sort it cleanly. And a lead whose answers clearly disqualify them, wrong service area, budget nowhere close to realistic, should get a polite, honest no rather than silence, because a fast no protects the business's reputation better than a lead that drifts for two weeks before anyone tells them it was never going to work.

The questions only pay off if someone is actually watching how the answers get used. A script that asks great questions but routes every single response to the same generic follow-up queue didn't really qualify anything, it just added five questions in front of the same undifferentiated pile.

## Lead Qualification Questions: The Questions People Actually Ask

**What are the five most important lead qualification questions?**
What's the actual problem, have they tried to fix it before, what's their rough budget, are they the decision-maker and how soon do they want it handled. Those five map onto the budget, authority, need and timeline categories most frameworks are built around.

**Is BANT still relevant for small businesses, not just enterprise sales?**
Yes. The categories, budget, authority, need, timeline, apply just as well to a homeowner picking a contractor as they do to an enterprise software deal. The questions just get phrased more casually for a small business lead than they would in a boardroom pitch.

**Can an AI agent ask lead qualification questions by text?**
It can, and the need and timeline questions are the easiest to automate well, since the answers are mostly factual. Budget works over text when it's phrased as a range rather than a direct ask. Authority is the one that still benefits most from a human catching tone and hesitation a text thread can miss.

**What's the difference between lead qualification questions and lead scoring?**
The questions are the actual script, what gets asked and in what words. [Lead scoring](/blog/ai-lead-qualification) is what happens to the answers afterward, the weighting and routing logic that decides whether a lead goes to a booking link, a rep's task list or a nurture sequence.

**Should every lead get asked the same qualification questions?**
The same five core questions work across most small businesses, but which one matters most shifts by industry. A roofing company leans hardest on urgency, while an insurance agency leans hardest on whether the lead is even in a coverage area before anything else gets asked.

**How fast should a lead get asked these questions after they reach out?**
As close to instantly as possible. [Speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead) research shows the odds of a real conversation fall off within minutes of first contact, so a qualification script that waits until a human is free to send it has already lost most of its value before the first question goes out.
