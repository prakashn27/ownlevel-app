SMS wins on speed and visibility almost every time, with texts getting read within minutes and answered within about ninety seconds on average, while email takes closer to ninety minutes to get a reply and gets opened by a much smaller share of recipients. Email still wins on cost at real volume and on anything that needs more than a sentence to explain. The channel that actually fits depends on the specific message, not on picking one winner for the whole business: a missed-call reply and a monthly newsletter aren't the same job, and treating them as the same job is how a lot of comparison guides on this topic go wrong.

## How Different Are SMS and Email Open Rates, Really?

The honest answer is that SMS doesn't have a true open rate at all, even though almost every guide on this topic quotes one. Standard text messages carry no tracking pixel, so the widely repeated 95 to 98 percent figure is actually a delivery and visibility estimate built from signals like delivery confirmation and message preview data, not a record of someone actually reading the text. EZTexting's own methodology notes that a message glanced at on a lock screen counts the same as one someone reads in full and replies to, which means that headline number measures how likely a text is to be seen, not how carefully it gets read.

Email open rates are the opposite problem: they are measurable, but the published averages vary by ten points or more depending on whose data you trust. Constant Contact reports roughly 31 percent across its own customer base, Mailchimp reports around 36 percent across its platform, and a narrower EZTexting comparison puts typical email open rates at 20 to 28 percent against SMS. None of those numbers are wrong exactly, they just measure different populations of senders, which is why picking a single "email open rate" to compare against SMS without naming the source is close to meaningless. The useful comparison isn't 98 percent against 30 percent. It is that SMS gets seen by almost everyone within minutes, while even a strong email list sees a meaningful share of recipients never open the message at all.

## How Much Faster Do People Respond to a Text Than an Email?

People respond to a text roughly sixty times faster than they respond to an email on average, with SMS replies landing around ninety seconds after delivery compared to roughly ninety minutes for email. About 90 percent of text messages get read within three minutes of arriving, and more granular 2026 data breaks that down further: 87 percent of consumers check a new text within fifteen minutes, 74 percent check within five minutes, and 23 percent check within a single minute of it landing on their phone.

That speed shows up in engagement, not just reading. SMS campaigns see reply rates around 45 percent and click-through rates between 21 and 36 percent, both far ahead of email's typical click-through range of 2 to 5 percent. The gap matters most for anything time-sensitive. A reminder that needs a yes or no answer, or a missed-call text that needs to catch someone before they call a competitor instead, depends on exactly the kind of near-immediate attention SMS gets and email almost never does.

## What Does Each Channel Actually Cost a Small Business?

SMS costs more per message, typically somewhere between one and five cents depending on the provider and message length, while email providers usually charge a flat monthly rate that covers unlimited sends, making email dramatically cheaper once volume climbs into the thousands. What most comparisons leave out is that SMS carries a second cost layer on top of the provider's own rate: US carriers add a per-segment surcharge, roughly $0.0025 to $0.0045 depending on which carrier the recipient is on, and any business running automated texts also has to register under A2P 10DLC, which runs about $46 to set up a brand plus roughly $10 a month to keep a campaign active.

None of that makes SMS a bad deal. A home services business sending a few hundred reminder and review-request texts a month will barely notice either the carrier surcharge or the registration fee once it is amortized across a year. Picture a landscaping company sending 400 appointment reminders and 150 review-request texts in a given month, 550 messages total. At three cents a message plus a four-tenths-of-a-cent carrier surcharge, that runs somewhere around nineteen dollars for the month, on top of the roughly ten-dollar monthly campaign fee that keeps the registration active, for a total closer to thirty dollars. The same 550 messages sent by email would cost close to nothing per send once the business is already paying a flat monthly rate for its email platform, but a meaningful share of those reminders and requests would also arrive too late to do their job, since email usually gets opened hours after SMS already got read and acted on.

The math changes for an agency running that same automation across a dozen white-labeled clients, where the carrier fees and the per-client registration cost both scale with the roster, and should get budgeted into onboarding rather than discovered after the fact. Email has no equivalent per-message carrier toll, so its real advantage shows up specifically at scale and specifically for content that doesn't need an answer in the next few minutes.

## Which Channel Should Handle Which Automated Message?

The channel that wins should change depending on what the business is actually trying to accomplish with that specific message, not get decided once for the whole account. A missed-call reply needs to land before the caller tries a competitor, which makes the speed of SMS worth the extra cost. A newsletter explaining a seasonal promotion needs more than 160 characters to make its case, which makes email's longer format worth the slower read.

| Automated message | Better default channel | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Missed-call text-back | SMS | The caller is deciding right now whether to try someone else; a ninety-second reply time matters more than cost |
| Appointment reminder | SMS, with an email backup | A reply confirming or rescheduling needs to happen fast, and a short text covers the one fact that matters: the date and time |
| Review request | SMS | Asking right after a job finishes, while the experience is fresh, beats a slower-opened email days later |
| [Lead follow-up](/blog/speed-to-lead) after a form fill | SMS first, email for detail | A fast first touch wins the lead; a longer email can follow with pricing or next steps once contact is made |
| Monthly newsletter or promotion | Email | There is no urgency to manufacture, and the format supports more than a one-line message |

The pattern across that table isn't that SMS always wins because it is faster. It is that SMS wins specifically when the message is short, time-sensitive, and needs a near-immediate reaction, while email wins when the content needs room to breathe or the timing isn't urgent at all. A business running [missed-call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back), [appointment reminders](/blog/no-show-appointments), and [review requests](/blog/google-reviews-legally) through the same platform is really running three different urgent, short messages that all belong on SMS, while its monthly update to past customers belongs on email instead.

## What Compliance Rules Actually Gate Each Channel in 2026?

SMS and email get regulated in two genuinely different ways, and mixing up those two ways is one of the more common mistakes in this comparison. SMS has a carrier-level gate: [A2P 10DLC registration](/blog/a2p-10dlc-compliance) decides whether an automated text gets delivered at all, and an unregistered sender's messages get rejected outright rather than delivered late. On top of that carrier gate sits TCPA, a federal consent law that makes sending without proper opt-in a legal liability carrying statutory damages of $500 to $1,500 per message, separate from whether the text was ever delivered in the first place.

Email has no equivalent carrier gate. There is no registration step that blocks an email from reaching an inbox the way an unregistered brand gets blocked on SMS. What email has instead is CAN-SPAM, a liability law rather than a delivery gate, which caps the maximum civil penalty at $53,088 per non-compliant email as of 2026, a figure that has climbed every year under federal inflation adjustments and stayed frozen at that level for the rest of this year. The practical difference matters more than the dollar figures: send an unregistered automated text and it simply never arrives, with no warning beyond a delivery log that quietly stops looking healthy. Send a CAN-SPAM-violating email and it still lands in the inbox, and the legal exposure only shows up later if someone complains or the FTC takes notice. SMS fails loud and immediately, while an email that violates CAN-SPAM keeps landing in inboxes with the problem staying invisible until someone complains, closer to a slow leak than a dropped call.

## Should an AI Agent Pick the Channel Automatically, or Should a Person Decide?

For the four moments in that table above, an AI agent choosing the channel by a fixed rule works fine without a person approving each send, the same [human on the loop model](/blog/human-in-the-loop-vs-on-the-loop) that already covers most routine automation at a small business. A missed call always gets a text, a review request always gets a text, and a monthly newsletter always goes by email, and none of that rule needs a human checking it message by message, because none of those four carries much downside if the channel choice is slightly off on any single send.

That changes the moment the content of the message, not the channel, starts committing the business to something specific. A lead follow-up that just confirms contact and promises a callback is routine regardless of whether it goes out by text or email. A follow-up that quotes an exact price or a guaranteed timeline is a different kind of message entirely, the kind that benefits from a person reviewing it before it goes out, independent of which channel carries it. The channel decision and the approval decision aren't the same question, and a business that only thinks about which channel to use, without separately asking whether a specific message needs a human's eyes first, is missing the part that actually protects it from a costly mistake.

An agency setting this up for a client is really making two separate rulebooks, not one. The first rulebook sorts messages by channel: short and urgent goes to SMS, longer and unhurried goes to email, with a handful of moments like appointment reminders defaulting to SMS with an email backup in case the text bounces. The second rulebook sorts the same messages by whether a person needs to see them first, and that sort hasn'thing to do with which channel carries the message. Keeping those two decisions separate, instead of assuming a fast channel also means a risky one, is what lets a business run most of its automated messages without a human in the middle while still catching the rare one that genuinely needs a second look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does SMS really have a 98 percent open rate?

Not in the way email does. SMS carries no tracking pixel, so the 95 to 98 percent figure is a delivery and visibility estimate, not a measured read rate the way email's open rate is. It is still a useful signal that texts get seen fast, just not literally an "open rate" in the email sense of the word.

### Is email actually cheaper than SMS?

At real volume, yes, since SMS costs roughly one to five cents per message plus a small per-segment carrier surcharge, while most email platforms charge a flat monthly rate covering unlimited sends. A business sending a few hundred texts a month barely notices the difference, but the gap grows once volume climbs into the thousands.

### Can the same message go out on both SMS and email at once?

It can, but it rarely needs to for the kind of automated alerts a small business runs. Sending the identical reminder on both channels usually just annoys the recipient without adding much, since the goal is picking the one channel that fits the message's urgency, not covering every base at once.

### Do I need to register for anything before texting customers automatically?

Yes, if the texts are automated or templated rather than typed by hand. A2P 10DLC registration is required for that traffic to be delivered at all, and it is separate from TCPA consent requirements, which govern whether you had permission to send in the first place rather than whether the carrier lets the message through.

### Which channel gets a faster response for appointment reminders?

SMS wins by a wide margin, with text replies averaging around ninety seconds compared to roughly ninety minutes for email, and a reminder that needs a quick confirmation or reschedule benefits directly from that speed.

### Should review requests go out by text or email?

Text wins as the first move, since a review request sent by SMS right after a job wraps up catches the customer while the experience is still fresh, and the faster read time means a higher share of customers see it the same day rather than days later in an inbox.

### What happens if a business just emails everything to avoid SMS costs?

The automation gets cheaper and noticeably less effective. [No-show rates](/blog/no-show-appointments) tend to climb when reminders arrive by email instead of text, since a reminder sitting unread for hours defeats the point of sending it three days before an appointment, and a review request that lands in an inbox days after the job finished gets ignored far more often than one sent by text within the hour. The savings on the message itself end up costing more in missed appointments and skipped reviews than the SMS fees would have.

## Match the Channel to the Moment, Not to a Default

The businesses that get the most out of SMS and email aren't the ones that picked a favorite channel and ran everything through it. They are the ones that looked at each specific automated message, the missed-call reply, the reminder, the review request, the monthly update, and matched the channel to what that message actually needed: speed, cost, length, or none of the above in a hurry. SMS earns its higher cost on anything urgent and short. Email earns its place on anything that can wait a day and needs more room to explain itself.
